Synthetic Data Generation Notes

Synthetic Fact Dataset: Design, Implementation, and Current Results

1. Objective

The goal of this dataset is to support a controlled experiment on cross-lingual factual knowledge transfer.

The central question is whether factual knowledge that becomes retrievable in Turkish after Turkish adaptation was:

  • transferred from knowledge previously learned in English, or
  • relearned from factual information appearing in the Turkish adaptation data.

To study this question, we need facts whose source and exposure history are fully known. Real-world facts are not suitable for this purpose because the model may already have encountered them during its original pretraining.

For this reason, we created a controlled synthetic biography dataset inspired by the bioS methodology.

The dataset uses synthetic subjects combined with controlled professions, cities, universities, and employers. The relationships between these subjects and objects are newly generated, even when some of the objects themselves are real-world entities.

2. General Dataset Structure

The first full dataset contains:

  • 5,000 unique synthetic subjects
  • 4 factual relations per subject
  • 20,000 factual relations in total

The four relations are:

  1. profession
  1. born_in
  1. studied_at
  1. works_at

An example subject profile is:

  • Leran Dovik is a teacher.
  • Leran Dovik was born in Bristol.
  • Leran Dovik studied at Northbridge University.
  • Leran Dovik works at Marston College.

Each subject is stored as one row in a canonical CSV file. The generation pipeline then expands each subject profile into four separate factual records.

This one-row-per-subject structure keeps all subject-level metadata in one place and simplifies dataset management.

4. Source Data

The profiles are generated from manually prepared single-line source lists.

The source files contain:

  • English first names
  • Turkish first names
  • English surnames
  • Turkish surnames
  • English cities
  • Turkish cities
  • English companies
  • Turkish companies
  • English universities
  • Turkish universities
  • aligned English and Turkish profession lists

The profession lists also contain popularity scores.

An example profession entry is:

Footballer — 100
Actor — 99
Warehouse worker — 29
Cleaner — 20

The English and Turkish profession files are aligned by line position. This ensures that every English profession has the correct Turkish equivalent and the same popularity score.

Before dataset generation, all source files are validated.

The validation process:

  • removes empty lines
  • removes exact duplicates
  • checks required files
  • validates profession scores
  • verifies English–Turkish profession alignment
  • reports the cleaned size of every source list

For example, many Turkish first-name and surname entries were duplicates or multi-component entries. These were removed before subject generation.

5. Synthetic Name Generation

The dataset contains:

  • 2,500 English-like names
  • 2,500 Turkish-like names

Each subject name consists of exactly:

  • one first name
  • one surname

English-like names are generated only from English first-name and surname pools.

Turkish-like names are generated only from Turkish first-name and surname pools.

The two component types are not mixed.

This prevents combinations such as an English first name with a Turkish surname and keeps the linguistic name categories controlled.

All names are normalized to natural casing.

For example:

LEONTYNE BUCK → Leontyne Buck
ÖMER ÜZÜM → Ömer Üzüm

Turkish characters are preserved.

All 5,000 full-name combinations are unique.

6. Name Rarity

The source lists are treated as approximate frequency-ranked lists. Earlier entries are assumed to be more common, while later entries are treated as less common.

Using the relative position of the first name and surname in their source lists, each full name receives a rarity category:

  • common
  • medium
  • rare

The final distribution is:

  • 2,000 common names
  • 1,750 medium-rarity names
  • 1,250 rare names

This allows us to test whether facts connected to more distinctive or rarer names are learned or transferred differently from facts connected to common names.

The rarity distribution is controlled independently of:

  • name type
  • experimental branch
  • subject popularity

7. Subject Popularity

Every synthetic subject receives a popularity score and rank.

Profession popularity is used as the main signal because highly visible professions, such as football players, actors, and singers, are generally associated with more public textual exposure than less visible professions.

However, profession popularity does not fully determine subject popularity.

The score is generated using:

fame score =
0.75 × profession popularity
+
0.25 × deterministic random component

The random component prevents all people with the same profession from receiving the same popularity.

The 5,000 subjects are ranked from most popular to least popular.

The final distribution is:

  • top 10%: high popularity — 500 subjects
  • next 30%: medium popularity — 1,500 subjects
  • bottom 60%: low popularity — 3,000 subjects

This produces more low-popularity subjects than high-popularity subjects, reflecting a more realistic long-tail distribution.

8. Geographic Profile Design

Initially, birthplace, university, and employer were assigned independently. This produced profiles that were technically possible but often lacked geographic coherence.

For example, a person could be born in one country, study in another, and work in a third without any controlled reason.

To improve this, the dataset now uses six explicit geographic profile patterns:

  1. English-region domestic
  1. Turkish-region domestic
  1. English-region subject studying in the Turkish region
  1. Turkish-region subject studying in the English region
  1. English-region subject working in the Turkish region
  1. Turkish-region subject working in the English region

The distribution is:

  • 1,750 English domestic profiles
  • 1,750 Turkish domestic profiles
  • 375 English-region profiles with Turkish-region education
  • 375 Turkish-region profiles with English-region education
  • 375 English-region profiles with Turkish-region employment
  • 375 Turkish-region profiles with English-region employment

This creates mostly coherent domestic profiles while still including controlled study-abroad and work-abroad cases.

Name type remains independent

A Turkish-like name is not automatically assigned to a Turkish-region profile.

Similarly, an English-like name is not automatically assigned to an English-region profile.

This is important because otherwise name type and biography region would become correlated.

If all Turkish-like names had Turkish cities, universities, and employers, it would be impossible to determine whether an observed effect came from:

  • the linguistic form of the name, or
  • the broader Turkish context of the profile.

Therefore, English-like and Turkish-like names are distributed across all geographic profile patterns.

9. Object Assignment

Objects may be shared across many subjects.

For example, multiple subjects may have:

  • the same profession
  • the same birthplace
  • the same university
  • the same employer

Only the subject and the subject–relation association are unique.

This is intentional.

The model may already know objects such as London, İstanbul, Microsoft, or a profession such as teacher. However, it cannot already know the newly generated relationship:

Synthetic subject → real object

For example:

Leran Dovik → born_in → London

is still a new synthetic fact even if the model already knows the city of London.

Objects are assigned independently of name type to avoid unwanted shortcuts.

10. Relation-Specific Frequency

Every relation receives its own frequency category:

  • low
  • medium
  • high

The current exposure mapping is:

  • low: 3 training exposures
  • medium: 8 training exposures
  • high: 15 training exposures

Frequency is relation-specific.

For example, one person may have:

  • profession: high
  • employer: high
  • birthplace: medium
  • university: low

This reflects the idea that different facts about the same person do not appear equally often in text.

A famous football player’s profession and club may appear frequently, while the person’s birthplace or university may appear less often.

The final dataset contains substantially more low-frequency facts than high-frequency facts:

  • high-frequency facts: 689
  • medium-frequency facts: 3,799
  • low-frequency facts: 15,512

This distribution follows the intended long-tail structure.

The generated English training data currently contains:

  • 86,669 training records
  • 20,000 unique facts

The number of records is larger than the fact count because facts are expressed multiple times according to their frequency category.

11. Multi-Template Generation

The dataset uses a multi-template approach inspired by multiM.

Each relation has:

  • 10 English training templates
  • 10 Turkish repetition templates
  • at least 3 English probe templates
  • at least 3 Turkish probe templates

The purpose is to separate factual frequency from exact sentence repetition.

For example, the fact:

Corin Veylor → born_in → Telmora

may be expressed as:

  • Corin Veylor was born in Telmora.
  • The birthplace of Corin Veylor is Telmora.
  • Telmora is the birthplace of Corin Veylor.
  • Corin Veylor's place of birth is Telmora.

The fact remains the same, but its surface form changes.

This reduces the chance that the model only memorizes one exact sentence pattern.

The generator uses the fullname heuristic. Every sentence contains the complete subject name instead of pronouns such as he, she, or they.

This avoids coreference ambiguity and keeps every sentence directly connected to its canonical subject.

12. Experimental Branches

The dataset contains two experimental branches.

Branch A: transfer condition

For Branch A subjects:

  • all four facts appear in English training
  • none of the facts appear in Turkish repetition data

This is the main transfer condition.

If a Branch A fact becomes retrievable in Turkish after Turkish adaptation, the result cannot be explained by direct repetition of that fact in Turkish adaptation data.

Branch B: repetition condition

For Branch B subjects:

  • all four facts appear in English training
  • all four facts are repeated in Turkish adaptation data

This condition represents Turkish-side reaffirmation or possible relearning.

The dataset contains:

  • 2,500 Branch A subjects
  • 2,500 Branch B subjects

This produces:

  • 10,000 Branch A facts
  • 10,000 Branch B facts

Branch assignment is subject-level rather than relation-level.

This avoids partial leakage where one fact about a person is repeated in Turkish while another fact about the same person is intended to remain transfer-only.

Branch assignment is stratified across:

  • name type
  • name rarity
  • popularity
  • profession popularity

This prevents the branch variable from becoming correlated with another dataset property.

13. Probe Generation

Every fact receives:

  • one English probe record
  • one Turkish probe record

The probe is generated from the same canonical fact table, but probe questions use templates that are different from the declarative training statements.

For example:

Training sentence

Leran Dovik works as a teacher.

English probe

What is Leran Dovik's profession?

Turkish probe

Leran Dovik'in mesleği nedir?

The expected answer is taken directly from the canonical English or Turkish object.

This design provides:

  • 20,000 English probes
  • 20,000 Turkish probes

The probes will allow us to compare factual retrieval:

  • in English before Turkish adaptation
  • in Turkish before Turkish adaptation
  • in English after Turkish adaptation
  • in Turkish after Turkish adaptation
  • between transfer and repetition conditions

16. Current Limitation: Profession–Employer Compatibility

The main remaining data-quality issue concerns the relationship between professions and employers.

The generator initially reported:

  • 100% employer compatibility
  • 0% final fallback

However, this number includes 1,787 assignments that used a general-employer fallback.

Therefore, the more informative breakdown is:

  • approximately 64% direct or category-based matches
  • approximately 36% general-employer fallback
  • 0% unassigned employers

This means every subject receives an employer, but not every profession–employer combination is equally natural.

Examples of potentially weak combinations include:

  • basketball player working at Boeing
  • sports coach working at Goldman Sachs
  • sewer worker working at Qualcomm
  • police officer working at Tesla

These facts are still technically usable as synthetic associations, but they reduce the naturalness of the biography profiles.

The current company pools mainly contain large corporations. They do not contain enough:

  • hospitals
  • schools
  • sports clubs
  • public institutions
  • police departments
  • municipal organizations
  • construction companies
  • farms
  • airports
  • prisons

to support all 200 professions naturally.

Planned correction

The next focused improvement should either:

  1. expand employer source lists with more organization types, or
  1. restrict employer assignment to professions that can be represented naturally by the available employer pools.

This issue affects the semantic quality of the works_at relation, but it does not invalidate the subject generation, branch structure, frequency design, or the other three relations.

17. Current Status

The full technical pipeline is working.

Completed components include:

  • source-list validation
  • 5,000-subject generation
  • 20,000-fact expansion
  • name-type balancing
  • name-rarity assignment
  • popularity ranking
  • geographic profile generation
  • relation-specific frequencies
  • branch assignment
  • multilingual template generation
  • English and Turkish probing
  • automated validation and testing

The dataset is ready for pilot experiments and pipeline testing.

Before using it as the final thesis dataset, the remaining profession–employer compatibility issue should be addressed and the English and Turkish template banks should receive a final manual linguistic review.

After these checks, the next experimental stage will be:

  1. teach the synthetic facts in English,
  1. evaluate factual retrieval in English and Turkish,
  1. adapt the model using Turkish data,
  1. expose only Branch B facts during Turkish adaptation,
  1. evaluate both branches again,
  1. compare Turkish retrieval between transfer-only and repetition conditions.
